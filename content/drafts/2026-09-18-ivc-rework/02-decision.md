## What Are the Signs You Have Outgrown Cage Wash?

Any one of these is reason enough to take a serious look. If you recognize two or three, you probably already know the answer.

1. Your washer is near end of life, or a big repair quote just landed. Price the alternative before that capital is committed.
2. Wash throughput caps your census or study intake. New work is waiting on clean cages.
3. You have had a contamination investigation or repeated health-monitoring concerns. A cage that cycles back through a shared wash loop carries every weakness of that loop.
4. Growth is fast or hard to predict. Wash capacity comes in large, fixed steps, and demand rarely does.
5. A new quarantine, immunocompromised, gnotobiotic, or infectious-disease program is on the way, and it needs clean housing on day one.
6. A renovation or move will take wash capacity offline. Consumable caging keeps animals housed meanwhile and raises a fair question: do you need to rebuild it at all?
7. Turnover or ergonomic strain is wearing on your wash crew. Dropping cage washing removes a repetitive-strain task, and a third-party analysis found our system [reduces ergonomic risk](https://innovive.com/system-benefits/) [1].

## Which Research Programs Gain the Most From Consumable Caging?

Every facility benefits from dropping the wash loop, but programs where a health-status break is most expensive benefit most. We see the strongest fit in:

- Gnotobiotic and defined-flora colonies
- Immunocompromised and humanized models
- Infectious disease and quarantine work
- Oncology and patient-derived xenograft (PDX) studies
- High-value genetically engineered lines

Here is why. Every Research-Ready cage arrives irradiated, double-bagged, and guaranteed never to have housed another rodent [2]. No cage has been through a shared wash cycle. For [gnotobiotic housing](https://innovive.com/gnotobiotics/), we pair that animal-naive cage with dual HEPA filtration at the rack and cage level using the MVX3 lid, racks that run positive or negative airflow, and lids that create an airtight seal [3].

The microenvironment holds up, too. In a 2008 study with each cage type on a positive-pressure rack, ammonia in reusable cages began rising on day 4, reached about 50 ppm by day 5, and passed 150 ppm at the rear sampling port by day 9. The disposable cages stayed at or below 3.2 ppm [4].

A 2022 study that used our cages, lids, and Aquavive water found ammonia stayed below the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH) 8-hour exposure limit for people, 25 ppm, through day 21 for group-housed mice and day 30 for singly housed mice. Bioburden on cage accessories and in the water stayed acceptable up to 28 days after cage change [5]. Most of our mouse customers change cages every 14 days [6], so that is a wide margin.

## What Does Consumable Caging Change for Facilities and CROs?

Capacity starts moving with your orders instead of your equipment. If you need more cages next quarter, you order them. Nobody writes a capital request for a second washer.

Without fixed washroom infrastructure, you free up floor space and lower your upfront investment [1]. You can open a satellite, quarantine, or containment suite without building a wash area beside it. Our [cage-wash-free vivarium planning checklist](https://innovive.com/the-cage-wash-free-vivarium-a-facility-planning-checklist-to-reduce-cage-wash-infrastructure-and-capex/) walks through what that means for a new build or a renovation.

For contract research organizations (CROs), the change shows up in the sponsor conversation. When a sponsor asks how you protect their animals, you have a short answer: every cage is animal-naive and irradiated, and each lot has a Certificate of Irradiation (COI) you can look up by lot number and keep on file for study records and compliance audits [7]. Sponsors can verify that themselves.

## What Should You Compare Before You Decide?

Compare fully loaded cost per cage-week, not just purchase price. A durable reusable cage is a capital purchase before it houses a single animal. After that, every cycle needs labor, utilities, steam, floor space, equipment maintenance, and wash validation to get it clean again.

The NIH Design Requirements Manual shows the scale. Cage wash planning has to account for census, caging type, change frequency, schedule, staffing, and redundancy, and the space needs dedicated heated water or steam, drainage, allowances for equipment heat and humidity loads, and separation of clean and dirty sides [8]. You pay for all of that whether the washer runs full or half empty.

On the consumable side, count cages, bedding, water, and the pickup of used caging. That last piece is easy. Through InnoCycle, your team re-stacks used cages with the bedding still in them, and they are picked up on your schedule. Our closed-loop service collects and processes 100% of the returned plastic [9].

One of our customers put a figure on it. AVEO Oncology saved over $2 million by switching from washable caging, according to its [customer case study](https://innovive.com/case-studies/) [10].

Our view is simple: if a washer replacement quote is sitting on your desk, run this comparison before you sign it.

## FAQ

### What type of facility is the best fit for disposable IVC caging?

Facilities with no washroom, or one that is aging, undersized, or expensive to run. New biotechs in leased space, satellite and quarantine suites, and fast-growing programs fit especially well, as do high-biosecurity programs in any building.

### Which study types benefit most from consumable caging?

Gnotobiotic, immunocompromised, humanized, infectious disease, quarantine, oncology and PDX, and high-value genetically engineered models. These animals are the hardest to replace. A new, animal-naive cage at every change takes the shared wash loop out of the contamination picture.

### Does colony size decide whether consumable caging makes sense?

Not by itself. Size changes the totals, but the decision rests on cost per cage-week once labor, utilities, space, maintenance, and validation are included. We build that comparison with you.

### Can we start with one room?

Yes. Research-Ready caging needs no wash infrastructure, and Innorack runs off a standard outlet [6], so a pilot room can be running quickly. From there you can add rooms or cohorts at whatever pace suits your program.

### How does consumable IVC caging compare with static micro-isolators?

Static micro-isolators still depend on cage wash, and they have no individual ventilation. On Innorack, every cage gets its own HEPA-filtered airflow, and rodents housed in IVCs have the lowest risk of cage-to-cage infectious spread among common caging types [13]. Add a fresh, animal-naive cage at every change and the wash dependency goes away too.
