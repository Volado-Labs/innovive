# The True Cost of Disposable IVC Caging: How to Build a Business Case for Your Vivarium

The caging line in your budget shows only part of what reusable caging costs. The rest sits in other budgets. Facilities pays for the washer and its replacement. Utilities pays for the steam, the heated water, and the drains. Staffing pays for the wash crew, and nobody bills anyone for the square footage. Together they are usually the biggest part of the bill.

A proposal that compares caging invoices alone tells leadership the wrong story. Count everything per occupied cage-week and the case for consumable caging is strong. It gets stronger when your census is growing or your washer is close to replacement.

## Quick Answer

Measure the true cost of consumable IVC caging per occupied cage-week, not per cage. For reusable caging, count the cages plus washer capital, utilities, maintenance, validation, wash labor, and floor space. For consumable caging, count caging, freight, storage, and recycling. On that basis consumables make a strong case, strongest when a washer is due for replacement or growth would force new wash capacity.

## Why Is Cage Price the Wrong Comparison?

The caging invoice is the wrong place to look. Reusable caging does most of its spending in the wash room, one of the heaviest pieces of infrastructure in any vivarium.

The NIH Design Requirements Manual lists what cage wash planning has to account for: census, caging type, change frequency, schedule, staffing, and redundancy. It also calls for dedicated heated water or steam, drainage, allowances for equipment heat and humidity, and separation of clean and dirty areas [1]. None of it shows up on a caging invoice.

What usually sits in other budgets:

- Washer and autoclave capital, plus the replacement you will eventually fund
- Steam, heated water, drainage, and the ventilation that carries off equipment heat
- Service contracts, repairs, and wash validation
- The staff hours that keep the wash room running
- The floor space the wash room occupies

Our side of the ledger is shorter: the caging itself, freight, storage, and recycling at end of use. Because the Innovive system needs no fixed washroom infrastructure, it frees floor space and reduces upfront investment [2]. Model those savings alongside the [welfare and ergonomic benefits of the Innovive system](https://innovive.com/system-benefits/).

## What Belongs in a Cost per Occupied Cage-Week?

An occupied cage-week is one cage housing animals for one week. Add them up across a year from your census records, not rack capacity or your busiest day, and you have your denominator. Total every cost the caging decision changes, on both sides, and divide.

On the consumable side, count cages, lids, and accessories at your contract pricing, plus freight, receiving, and storage. Caging supplies keep for a year in original packaging in dry storage under 115 F (46 C) [3], so you can order on your own schedule.

Change frequency drives this line most. Most of our mouse users change Innocage every 14 days, and most rat users every 7 to 10 days [3]. Bottom-only changes, where you replace the bottom and keep the lid, feeder, and water bottle, lower consumption further [4].

On the reusable side, count the future spending you would avoid: utilities, service, validation, wash labor, replacement reusable cages, and the washer or autoclave replacement already in your capital plan. A washer bought ten years ago is a sunk cost. Its next five years of steam, service, and staffing are not.

Leave out anything that stays the same either way, like daily health checks.

## How Do Census Swings and Growth Change the Math?

A cage wash has to be sized for your peak. Consumable caging follows your actual census. Take an illustrative colony that hits 1,800 cages for six weeks and runs near 700 the rest of the year. The wash room has to be ready for 1,800 all year. Consumables get bought for what is on the racks.

Washroom costs are also step-fixed. They hold flat, then jump when you add a shift, a second washer, or a bigger wash room. That next step is where consumables usually win outright.

Break-even is plain arithmetic. Take the washroom costs you would avoid in a year and divide them by your annual occupied cage-weeks. Set that wash room cost per cage-week next to your consumable spend per occupied cage-week. Where the first meets or beats the second, switching pays. We run it with your own figures, because your utility rates, labor costs, and census decide it.

Two things push the result our way. The wash room is mostly fixed cost, so every week your census sits below peak makes each washed cage-week more expensive. And the next capacity step lands entirely in the avoided-cost column. That is why the case is strongest at washer replacement or a capacity expansion.

Give contamination its own line. A health-status break means repeated work and delayed studies, and your own history shows how often one happens. Our Research-Ready caging arrives irradiated, double-bagged, and animal-naive at every change [5]. We show that to leadership as a risk reduction, kept separate from the hard savings.

## How Do You Present the Case to Leadership?

Leadership wants to see where every number came from.

1. Collect the records. Utility bills, service contracts, timecards, purchasing history, census records, and the capital plan. Tie each input to its source document.
2. Run base, low, and high cases. Move the inputs that swing the result most: loaded labor rates, annual census, contract tier, and washer replacement timing.
3. Put a value on space. A wash room you never build or expand is room for housing, procedures, or research. Our [cage-wash-free vivarium planning checklist](https://innovive.com/the-cage-wash-free-vivarium-a-facility-planning-checklist-to-reduce-cage-wash-infrastructure-and-capex/) covers the infrastructure you can plan out.
4. Propose a pilot room. Start with one room or one cohort, track cage-weeks and labor hours, then scale. Many facilities bring consumables in this way. It hands leadership real numbers from your own building.

## FAQ

### How much does consumable IVC caging cost per cage-week?

It depends on volume, change frequency, and options like pre-bedded cages or pre-filled water. Most of our mouse users change cages every 14 days, so each cage covers two weeks of housing [3]. We can price your program at your actual census.

### Is consumable caging cheaper than reusable caging?

The fair comparison is full cost per occupied cage-week, with washer capital, utilities, validation, wash labor, and floor space on the reusable side. On that basis consumable caging makes a strong case. It is strongest when a washer is due for replacement or you need more wash capacity to grow.
