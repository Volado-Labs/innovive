# How to Configure an IVC Rack for Mice and Rats

You size an IVC rack from the animals outward. IVC stands for individually ventilated cage: a cage with its own filtered air supply and exhaust, held in a rack that moves the air. Count your peak census, choose a cage that meets space recommendations, work out the cage count, and only then pick rows, columns and sides. Here is the method, with real numbers and worked examples on our Innorack IVC racks.

> **Quick answer**
> To configure an IVC rack, work from your animal census to cages, then to racks. Choose a cage that meets space recommendations for your species and weight, set animals per cage, size rows and columns to your cage count and room, pick positive or negative pressure, and plan how cages get changed.

## Step 1. Start from your animal census

Size for the peak census you expect over the next two to three years, not today's. Count by species and expected adult weight, and keep breeding separate from holding, since mothers with litters rarely share a cage size with holding animals.

The census also tells you whether you need a mouse IVC cage system, a rat system or both. A mixed census usually means mixed racks, because each Innorack is built around its own cage bottoms. The one overlap is the M-BTM-XL mouse cage, which runs on the IVC Rat 3.5.

Most U.S. programs work from the minimums in the *Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals* (8th edition) [1]:

| Animal | Weight | Floor area per animal | Minimum cage height |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mice in groups | Under 10 g | 6 in² (38.7 cm²) | 5 in (12.7 cm) |
| | Up to 15 g | 8 in² (51.6 cm²) | 5 in (12.7 cm) |
| | Up to 25 g | 12 in² (77.4 cm²) | 5 in (12.7 cm) |
| | Over 25 g | 15 in² or more (96.7 cm² or more) | 5 in (12.7 cm) |
| Mouse female with litter | | 51 in² (330 cm²) for the housing group | 5 in (12.7 cm) |
| Rats in groups | Under 100 g | 17 in² (109.6 cm²) | 7 in (17.8 cm) |
| | Up to 200 g | 23 in² (148.35 cm²) | 7 in (17.8 cm) |
| | Up to 300 g | 29 in² (187.05 cm²) | 7 in (17.8 cm) |
| | Up to 400 g | 40 in² (258.0 cm²) | 7 in (17.8 cm) |
| | Up to 500 g | 60 in² (387.0 cm²) | 7 in (17.8 cm) |
| | Over 500 g | 70 in² or more (451.5 cm² or more) | 7 in (17.8 cm) |
| Rat female with litter | | 124 in² (800 cm²) for the housing group | 7 in (17.8 cm) |

Height runs from cage floor to cage top. The *Guide* calls these figures minimums and leaves adjustments to the IACUC [1][2]. Your IACUC or AAALAC program may be stricter, so check local policy first.

## Step 2. Choose the cage size, then animals per cage

Divide a cage's floor space by the per-animal figure for adult weight and round down. That is a ceiling, not a target. The *Guide* does not count floor covered by water containers or enrichment, and your IACUC sets the final density [2].

## Step 3. How many cages do you need?

**Cages = (peak census ÷ animals per cage, rounded up) + buffer**

The buffer covers what a headcount misses, such as new breeding setups and animals separated for fighting or health. Quarantine and change-day spares need positions too. Set it from your own history.

> **Worked mini-example**
> 600 adult mice at 4 per cage = 150 cages.
> With an illustrative 15% buffer: 150 × 1.15 = 172.5, so plan 173 positions.
> The 15% shows the method. It is not a recommendation. [CONFIRM: Innovive recommended buffer]

## Step 4. Choose the rack: capacity, sides and layout

A single-sided rack sits against a wall and is worked from one aisle. A double-sided rack needs aisles on both faces and holds twice the cages for the same rows and columns. In most holding rooms the walls make this call before the census does.

Rows also set the height of the top row. If technicians will change cages at shoulder height all day, two shorter racks can beat one tall one.

## Step 5. Should the rack run positive or negative pressure?

An IVC rack pushes filtered air into each cage and draws it back out. The *Guide* says IVCs can meet the animals' ventilation needs without raising room ventilation, though the room still has to handle heat, odor and gases released from the cages [3].

Pressure mode decides which way air escapes. Positive pressure pushes air out of the cage and protects the animals. Negative pressure pulls air in and protects people and the room.

| Mode | Protects | Typical uses |
|---|---|---|
| Positive | The animals | Immunocompromised lines, gnotobiotic programs, high-value colonies |
| Negative | People and the room | Allergen control, and biohazard or infectious studies |

The allergen research supports that split. In one study, all six IVC systems tested contained mouse urinary allergen more than 250-fold at negative pressure; at positive pressure, unsealed racks leaked and the sealed system did not [4]. A later study found the lowest room allergen levels with sealed IVCs in either mode and unsealed IVCs under negative pressure [5]. So ask any vendor, us included, whether its cages count as sealed. [CONFIRM: whether Innocage IVC lids are sealed in the sense used in these studies]

HEPA on supply protects the animals, and HEPA on exhaust protects the room. Every Innorack filters both.

Then decide where exhaust goes. The *Guide* allows well-filtered IVC exhaust back into the room but calls ducting it to building exhaust generally preferable [3]. Innoracks run on their own blowers and need no room exhaust connection, which keeps them mobile. An optional connector ties a rack to facility HVAC if you want odor and humidity out of the room.

## Step 6. Plan the cage-change and wash workflow

In our view this is where most rack plans go wrong, and it has little to do with the rack. Who changes cages, how often, and where do the dirty ones go?

Most users of our Innocage IVC Mouse change cages every 14 days; most rat users change every 7 to 10. The *Guide* accepts longer IVC intervals when you verify the cage environment, for example by measuring ammonia and CO₂ [6].

Washable caging turns every change into a loop. Dirty cages go to the wash room to be cleaned, sterilized and dried, then come back. The loop needs washers, water, energy, storage and people. The same allergen study found staff exposure highest during cage changing and emptying soiled cages [5].

Disposable caging ends the loop at your dock. Our cages arrive irradiated and double-bagged, and none has ever housed another animal. After use they go back into their bags with the bedding still inside, then into gaylords for pickup. InnoCycle, our closed-loop recycling program, separates the bedding and recycles the plastic, targeting zero landfill. [See how the system works](https://innovive.com/how-it-works/).

| | Washable caging | Disposable Innocage caging |
|---|---|---|
| Cage wash | Bottoms, lids and bottles | None for bottoms and lids; rat wire bars still go through a washer, autoclave or hand wipe |
| Change steps | Dump bedding, wash, sterilize, dry, restock | Open the bag, set up, change, re-stack the used cage |
| Storage | Clean and dirty cages plus the wash room | New caging ([CONFIRM: caging shelf life, correction pending from Innovive]; kept dry, under 115°F/46°C) and a recycling area, often about 10' × 10' |
| Contamination risk | Depends on wash and sterilization | Each cage arrives irradiated and unused |
| Up-front cost | Racks, cages, washers, wash room | Racks; no wash room |
| Running cost | Water, detergent, energy, labor, repairs | A cost per cage, plus a fee if you use our recycling program |

That last row is the honest trade-off: disposable caging swaps capital and wash labor for a cost on every cage you open. Which is cheaper depends on your census, labor rates and whether you already own a working cage wash. We would rather run that math with you than assert it.

## FAQ

### What is an IVC rack?

A rack that holds individually ventilated cages and gives each one filtered supply air and its own exhaust. Innoracks HEPA-filter both and run on their own blowers.

### How many cages fit on an IVC rack?

It depends on the rack and its rows and columns. The Innorack IVC Mouse 3.5 holds up to 192 cages, the IVC Rat 3.5 up to 80, the Tall IVC Rat up to 60 and the Tall IVC Rat XL up to 24.

### Should my IVC rack run positive or negative pressure?

Positive when you are protecting the animals, as in immunocompromised or gnotobiotic work. Negative when you are protecting people and the room, as in allergen control or infectious studies. Every Innorack runs both.

### What is the difference between IVC and static caging?

An IVC gets a direct, continuous exchange of air from the rack. A static cage relies on passive ventilation from the room, and the *Guide* notes that filter-topped cages without forced air may need more frequent sanitation [6].

### Can I use my existing cages on a new IVC rack?

The Innorack is designed for Innocage caging, so plan rack and cages as one system. [CONFIRM: wording on third-party cages and racks] If you are moving from another system, we can plan the changeover with you.

### How much does an IVC rack cost?

It depends on capacity, sides, model and options, and on whether you run washable or disposable caging. We provide sized quotes rather than list prices.

### How often should IVC rack filters be changed?

It varies by model, room conditions and use. [CONFIRM: Innorack filter replacement interval] Whoever you buy from, get the interval in writing before you order.

### How do I choose an IVC cage system?

Start with the animals, not the rack. Size cages to your census and the *Guide* minimums (Steps 1 to 3), then choose racks, pressure and workflow (Steps 4 to 6). If you house mice and rats, plan the two systems side by side, and our worksheet walks you through it.

### What size rat rack system do I need?

Count rat cages first, at 2 rats per R-BTM-H up to 500 g each. Our Innorack IVC Rat 3.5 holds up to 80 cages with 7" of headroom. Need 9"? The Tall IVC Rat holds up to 60, and the Tall IVC Rat XL holds up to 24 extra-large cages.
